# Part One: Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

## Chapter 2: The Big Secret of Dealing With People

There is only one way under high heaven to get anybody to do anything. Did you ever stop to think of that? Yes, just one way. And that is by **making the other person want to do it.**

Remember, there is no other way.

Of course, you can make someone want to give you his watch by sticking a revolver in his ribs. You can make your employees give you cooperation—until your back is turned—by threatening to fire them. You can make a child do what you want it to do by a whip or a threat. But these crude methods have sharply undesirable repercussions.

The only way I can get you to do anything is by giving you what you want.

### What Do People Want?

Sigmund Freud said that everything you and I do springs from two motives:

1. The sex urge
2. The desire to be great

John Dewey, one of America's most profound philosophers, phrased it a bit differently. Dr. Dewey said that the deepest urge in human nature is **"the desire to be important."** Remember that phrase: *"the desire to be important."* It is significant. You are going to hear a lot about it in this book.

What do you want? Not many things, but the few that you do wish, you crave with an insistence that will not be denied. Some of the things most people want include:

1. Health and the preservation of life
2. Food
3. Sleep
4. Money and the things money will buy
5. Life in the hereafter
6. Sexual gratification
7. The well-being of our children
8. **A feeling of importance**

Almost all these wants are usually gratified—all except one. But there is one longing—almost as deep, almost as imperious, as the desire for food or sleep—which is seldom gratified. It is what Freud calls *"the desire to be great."* It is what Dewey calls the *"desire to be important."*

---

### The Gnawing Hunger for Appreciation

Lincoln once began a letter saying: *"Everybody likes a compliment."*

William James said: *"The deepest principle in human nature is the craving to be appreciated."* He didn't speak, mind you, of the "wish" or the "desire" or the "longing" to be appreciated. He said the **"craving"** to be appreciated.

Here is a gnawing and unfaltering human hunger, and the rare individual who honestly satisfies this heart hunger will hold people in the palm of his or her hand and *"even the undertaker will be sorry when he dies."*

The desire for a feeling of importance is one of the chief distinguishing differences between mankind and the animals.

> **An Illustration:**
> When I was a farm boy out in Missouri, my father bred fine Duroc-Jersey hogs and pedigreed white-faced cattle. We used to exhibit them at country fairs and livestock shows throughout the Middle West. We won first prizes by the score. My father pinned his blue ribbons on a sheet of white muslin, and when visitors came, he and I would hold opposite ends of the sheet to exhibit them.
> The hogs didn't care about the ribbons they had won. But Father did. These prizes gave him a **feeling of importance.**

If our ancestors hadn't had this flaming urge for a feeling of importance, civilization would have been impossible. Without it, we should have been just about like animals.

---

### How the Desire for Importance Drives Action

* **Abraham Lincoln:** Led an uneducated, poverty-stricken grocery clerk to study law books he found in the bottom of a 50-cent barrel of plunder.
* **Charles Dickens:** Inspired him to write his immortal novels.
* **Sir Christopher Wren:** Inspired him to design his symphonies in stone.
* **John D. Rockefeller:** Made him amass millions that he never spent.
* **Your Neighbors:** Makes the richest family in town build a house far too large for its requirements, wear the latest styles, drive the latest cars, and talk about their brilliant children.
* **Youth Gangs:** Lures many boys and girls into criminal activities. E. P. Mulrooney, onetime police commissioner of New York, noted that the average young criminal is filled with ego—his first request after arrest is for lurid newspapers that make him out a hero alongside sports stars and celebrities.

#### Character is Defined by How You Seek Importance

> *"If you tell me how you get your feeling of importance, I'll tell you what you are. That determines your character."*

* **John D. Rockefeller** got his feeling of importance by giving money to erect a modern hospital in Peking, China, to care for millions of poor people.
* **John Dillinger** got his feeling of importance by being a bandit, bank robber, and killer—proudly announcing to farm families: *"I'm not going to hurt you, but I'm Dillinger!"*

---

### Historical Examples of the Struggle for Importance

* **George Washington** wanted to be called *"His Mightiness, the President of the United States."*
* **Christopher Columbus** pleaded for the title *"Admiral of the Ocean and Viceroy of India."*
* **Catherine the Great** refused to open letters not addressed to *"Her Imperial Majesty."*
* **Mary Todd Lincoln** turned upon Mrs. Grant in the White House and shouted: *"How dare you be seated in my presence until I invite you!"*
* **Antarctic Explorers:** Millionaires helped finance Admiral Byrd's 1928 expedition on the condition that icy mountain ranges be named after them.
* **Victor Hugo** aspired to have the city of Paris renamed in his honor.
* **William Shakespeare** tried to add luster to his name by procuring a coat of arms for his family.

#### Invalids and Insanity as an Escape for Attention

People sometimes become invalids to win sympathy, attention, and a feeling of importance:

* **Mrs. McKinley** forced her husband, the President, to recline beside her bed for hours, soothing her to sleep while neglecting affairs of state.
* **A Patient of Mary Roberts Rinehart:** A young woman took to her bed for 10 years, forcing her mother to serve her. When her mother died of exhaustion, the "invalid" got up, dressed, and resumed living.

Authorities state that people may actually go insane to find the feeling of importance denied to them in reality. A head physician at a major psychiatric hospital shared:

> "I have a patient whose marriage was a tragedy. She wanted love, children, and social prestige, but life blasted her hopes. Her husband didn't love her and forced her to serve his meals in his room. She went insane. In her imagination, she divorced her husband, married into English aristocracy as 'Lady Smith,' and believes she bears a new baby every night.
> Life wrecked her dream ships on the rocks of reality; but in the fantasy of insanity, all her ships race into port. If I could stretch out my hand and restore her sanity, I wouldn't do it. She's much happier as she is."

### The Executive Secret of Charles Schwab

One of the first people in American business to be paid a salary of over a million dollars a year was **Charles Schwab** (chosen by Andrew Carnegie in 1921 to head United States Steel, and later Bethlehem Steel).

Why was Schwab paid over $3,000 a day? Because he was a genius in steel manufacturing? No—Schwab admitted many men working under him knew more about steel manufacturing than he did. He was paid for his **ability to deal with people.**

#### Schwab’s Philosophy on Human Management:

> *"I consider my ability to arouse enthusiasm among my people the greatest asset I possess, and the way to develop the best that is in a person is by appreciation and encouragement."*
> *"There is nothing else that so kills the ambitions of a person as criticisms from superiors. I never criticize anyone. I believe in giving a person incentive to work. So I am anxious to praise but loath to find fault. If I like anything, I am hearty in my approbation and lavish in my praise."*

Average people do the exact opposite: if they don't like something, they bawl out subordinates; if they like it, they say nothing.

#### Andrew Carnegie & John D. Rockefeller

* **Carnegie** praised associates publicly and privately. For his tombstone, he wrote: *"Here lies one who knew how to get around him men who were cleverer than himself."*
* **Rockefeller** praised partner Edward T. Bedford after a bad South American investment lost $1 million—congratulating him for saving 60% of the capital: *"That's splendid. We don't always do as well as that upstairs."*

### Appreciation in Daily Life and Relationships

* **Runaway Spouses:** Studies reveal the primary reason wives (and husbands) leave home is a **"lack of appreciation."**
* **The 6 Red Roses:** A husband asked to list six things to change about his wife sent her six red roses instead with a note: *"I can't think of six things I would like to change about you. I love you the way you are."* She greeted him at the door in tears of joy.
* **Florenz Ziegfeld:** Broadway's legendary producer transformed chorus girls into glamorous stars by showering them with gallantry, higher pay, and American Beauty roses on opening night.
* **Nourishing Self-Esteem:** We fast from food, yet let family, friends, and employees go weeks or years without nourishing their self-esteem with words of praise. As actor Alfred Lunt put it: *"There is nothing I need so much as nourishment for my self-esteem."*
* **Stevie Wonder:** A Detroit schoolteacher praised young, blind Stevie Morris for using his remarkable ears to locate a lost classroom mouse. That single act of appreciation launched his music career.
* **Pamela Dunham’s Janitor:** Instead of joining employees in ridiculing a poor janitor, a supervisor praised him publicly whenever he did a good job. He soon transformed into an efficient, excellent worker.

---

### Appreciation vs. Flattery

Some may dismiss praise as shallow "flattery" or "bear oil." But flattery rarely works on discerning people because it is insincere, shallow, and selfish.

| Appreciation | Flattery |
| --- | --- |
| Sincere | Insincere |
| Comes from the heart | Comes from the teeth out |
| Unselfish | Selfish |
| Universally admired | Universally condemned |

* **General Alvaro Obregon:** *"Don't be afraid of enemies who attack you. Be afraid of the friends who flatter you."*
* **King George V’s Maxim:** *"Teach me neither to proffer nor receive cheap praise."*
* **Definition of Flattery:** *"Flattery is telling the other person precisely what he thinks about himself."*
* **Ralph Waldo Emerson:** *"Use what language you will, you can never say anything but what you are."*

When we stop thinking about ourselves (which occupies 95% of our time) and focus on the other person's good points, we don't need cheap flattery.

---

### A New Way of Life

* **In Daily Encounters:** Compliment the chef on a great meal; praise a tired salesperson for unusual courtesy; encourage children when they achieve something new.
* **Leave Sparks of Gratitude:** They set small flames of friendship that act as rose beacons on your journey.

> **A Motto to Live By:**
> *"I shall pass this way but once; any good, therefore, that I can do or any kindness that I can show to any human being, let me do it now. Let me not defer nor neglect it, for I shall not pass this way again."*

Emerson said: *"Every man I meet is my superior in some way. In that, I learn of him."*

Let's cease thinking of our accomplishments and wants. Let's figure out the other person's good points, forget flattery, and give honest, sincere appreciation.

> **Give honest and sincere appreciation.**
