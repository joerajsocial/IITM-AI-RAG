# Part One: Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

## Chapter 3: "He Who Can Do This Has The Whole World With Him. He Who Cannot Walks A Lonely Way"

I often went fishing up in Maine during the summer. Personally I am very fond of strawberries and cream, but I have found that for some strange reason, fish prefer worms. So when I went fishing, I didn't think about what I wanted. I thought about what *they* wanted. I didn't bait the hook with strawberries and cream. Rather, I dangled a worm or a grasshopper in front of the fish and said: *"Wouldn't you like to have that?"*

Why not use the same common sense when fishing for people?

That is what Lloyd George, Great Britain's Prime Minister during World War I, did. When someone asked him how he managed to stay in power after the other wartime leaders—Wilson, Orlando and Clemenceau—had been forgotten, he replied that if his staying on top might be attributed to any one thing, it would be to his having learned that **it was necessary to bait the hook to suit the fish.**

Why talk about what we want? That is childish. Absurd. Of course, you are interested in what you want. You are eternally interested in it. But no one else is. The rest of us are just like you: we are interested in what we want.

> **The Secret of Influence:**
> The only way on earth to influence other people is to **talk about what they want and show them how to get it.**

Remember that tomorrow when you are trying to get somebody to do something. If, for example, you don't want your children to smoke, don't preach at them, and don't talk about what you want; but show them that cigarettes may keep them from making the basketball team or winning the hundred-yard dash.

This is a good thing to remember regardless of whether you are dealing with children or calves or chimpanzees.

> **An Illustration:**
> One day Ralph Waldo Emerson and his son tried to get a calf into the barn. But they made the common mistake of thinking only of what they wanted: Emerson pushed and his son pulled. But the calf was doing just what they were doing; he was thinking only of what *he* wanted; so he stiffened his legs and stubbornly refused to leave the pasture.
> The Irish housemaid saw their predicament. She couldn't write essays and books; but she had more horse sense—or calf sense—than Emerson had. She thought of what the calf wanted; so she put her maternal finger in the calf's mouth and let the calf suck her finger as she gently led him into the barn.

### Understanding Human Desire

Every act you have ever performed since the day you were born was performed because you wanted something. How about the time you gave a large contribution to the Red Cross? Yes, that is no exception to the rule. You gave the Red Cross the donation because you wanted to lend a helping hand; you wanted to do a beautiful, unselfish, divine act. *"Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me."*

If you hadn't wanted that feeling more than you wanted your money, you would not have made the contribution. Of course, you might have made the contribution because you were ashamed to refuse or because a customer asked you to do it. But one thing is certain: you made the contribution because you wanted something.

Harry A. Overstreet, in his illuminating book *Influencing Human Behavior*, said:

> *"Action springs out of what we fundamentally desire... and the best piece of advice which can be given to would-be persuaders, whether in business, in the home, in the school, in politics, is: **First, arouse in the other person an eager want. He who can do this has the whole world with him. He who cannot walks a lonely way.**"*

### Case Studies in Arousing Desire

#### 1. Andrew Carnegie and the Yale Nephews

Andrew Carnegie, who started at two cents an hour and gave away $365 million, learned early that the only way to influence people is to talk in terms of what the other person wants.

His sister-in-law was worried sick over her two sons at Yale, who neglected to write home. Carnegie wagered $100 that he could get a reply by return mail without even asking for it. He wrote them a chatty letter, mentioning casually in a postscript that he was enclosing a $5 bill for each—but deliberately omitted the money.

Replies came back immediately thanking "Dear Uncle Andrew"—and inquiring about the missing cash.

#### 2. Stan Novak and the Kindergarten Readiness

When Stan Novak’s son Tim refused to go to kindergarten, screaming on the living room floor, Stan avoided punishing him. Instead, he asked: *"If I were Tim, why would I be excited about going to kindergarten?"*

Stan and his family began finger-painting on the kitchen table, having a wonderful time. When Tim begged to join, Stan told him, *"Oh no! You have to go to kindergarten first to learn how to finger-paint."* The next morning, Tim was awake early, waiting in a chair so he wouldn't be late.

### Applied Negotiation: The Ballroom Rent Strategy

When Dale Carnegie was notified that his lecture hall rent would increase by 300% after tickets were already printed, he didn't argue or complain. He created an **Advantages vs. Disadvantages** list strictly from the *hotel manager’s perspective*:

```
+------------------------------------------+------------------------------------------+
| Advantages (for the Hotel)               | Disadvantages (for the Hotel)            |
+------------------------------------------+------------------------------------------+
| • Ballroom freed up for profitable       | • Revenue from Carnegie lost completely  |
|   dances and conventions.                |   (forced to move elsewhere).            |
|                                          | • Loss of advertising to cultured crowds |
|                                          |   (worth $5,000 in newspaper ads).       |
+------------------------------------------+------------------------------------------+

```

He handed this list to the manager and asked him to weigh the options. The next day, Carnegie received a letter reducing the rent increase from **300% down to 50%**.

> **Henry Ford’s Secret of Success:**
> *"If there is any one secret of success, it lies in the ability to get the other person's point of view and see things from that person's angle as well as from your own."*

### Common Mistakes in Communication

#### Case Study A: The Self-Centered Agency Letter

A letter from an advertising agency executive to radio station managers demonstrates what *not* to do:

* **What the Agency Wrote:** *"The company desires to retain its position in advertising agency leadership..."*
* **The Manager's Internal Reaction:** Nobody cares about what *your* company desires. I am worried about my mortgage, health, and station issues.
* **What the Agency Wrote:** *"We desire to service our accounts with the last word on information... Put us on your preferred list..."*
* **The Manager's Internal Reaction:** You brag about your success, make me feel small, demand special service, and don't even say "please."

#### Case Study B: The Freight Terminal Letter

A freight terminal manager sent a letter complaining about late afternoon truck arrivals causing congestion and overtime.

* **The Error:** The letter focused entirely on the terminal's operational headaches, burying the customer's actual benefit (faster processing) at the very end.
* **The Solution:** A rewritten version placed the customer first: thanking them for 14 years of business, explaining how morning deliveries prevent truck delays, and letting drivers get home early for dinner.

#### Case Study C: The Strategic Job Application Letter

Barbara Anderson applied to 12 banks in Phoenix by focusing purely on what she could do for *them*:

> *"My ten years of bank experience should be of interest to a rapidly growing bank like yours... I am sure I can contribute to your growth and profit."*

Eleven out of the twelve banks invited her for an interview because she framed her experience around *their* goals.

### Win-Win Application Across Industries

* **Sales (John vs. Carl):** Two insurance agents approached the same firm. Carl casually mentioned a new policy. John presented it enthusiastically as a special opportunity, created an eager want, and closed the deals immediately.
* **Operations (Shell Oil):** Sales representative Mike Whidden couldn't motivate a station manager to clean up a run-down station until he took him to visit a pristine, top-performing branch. Seeing the modern setup inspired an eager want in the manager to upgrade his own location, driving up sales for both of them.
* **Public Speaking:** A student tried to recruit colleagues for basketball by complaining that *he* wanted to play and didn't want to get another black eye alone. He failed because he didn't highlight their benefits: energy, fitness, fun, and teamwork.

### Arousing Desire in Children

#### 1. Encouraging Proper Eating

When a three-year-old boy refused to eat, his parents stopped nagging (*"Father wants you to grow up big"*). Instead, they tied eating to his desire to beat up the neighborhood bully who kept stealing his tricycle. The dietary issues vanished overnight.

#### 2. Bed-Wetting and Pride

To resolve bed-wetting, the parents focused on what the boy wanted: grown-up pajamas and his own bed. They took him shopping, let him pick his bed, made him feel important, and praised him. His pride prevented him from wetting his own bed again.

#### 3. Breakfast Cereal and Self-Expression

When a toddler refused her breakfast cereal, her parents let her stir the pot on a chair to make it herself. Experiencing self-expression and importance, she ate two full helpings without any coaxing.

> **William Winter on Human Motivation:**
> *"Self-expression is the dominant necessity of human nature."*

## In a Nutshell: Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

* **Principle 1:** Don't criticize, condemn or complain.
* **Principle 2:** Give honest and sincere appreciation.
* **Principle 3:** **Arouse in the other person an eager want.**
