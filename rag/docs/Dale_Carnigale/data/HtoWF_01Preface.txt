Preface to Revised Edition
How to Win Friends and Influence People was first published in 1937 in an edition of only five thousand copies. Neither Dale Carnegie nor the publishers, Simon and Schuster, anticipated more than this modest sale.

To their amazement, the book became an overnight sensation, and edition after edition rolled off the presses to keep up with the increasing public demand. How to Win Friends and Influence People took its place in publishing history as one of the all-time international best-sellers. It touched a nerve and filled a human need that was more than a faddish phenomenon of post-Depression days, as evidenced by its continued and uninterrupted sales into the eighties, almost half a century later.

Cultural Impact & Legacy
Dale Carnegie used to say that it was easier to make a million dollars than to put a phrase into the English language. "How to Win Friends and Influence People" became such a phrase—quoted, paraphrased, parodied, and used in innumerable contexts from political cartoons to novels.

Reach: Translated into almost every known written language.

Longevity: Each generation has discovered it anew and found it relevant.

Why Revise a Classic?
Which brings us to the logical question: Why revise a book that has proven and continues to prove its vigorous and universal appeal? Why tamper with success?

To answer that, we must realize that Dale Carnegie himself was a tireless reviser of his own work during his lifetime:

A Living Textbook: The book was written to be used as a textbook for his courses in Effective Speaking and Human Relations, which are still taught today.

Continuous Adaptation: Until his death in 1955, Carnegie constantly improved and revised the course itself to make it applicable to evolving public needs.

Updating for the Era: Carnegie was deeply sensitive to modern trends and updated his book on Effective Speaking several times. Had he lived longer, he himself would have revised How to Win Friends and Influence People to better reflect changing times.

The Need for Modernization: Many names of prominent figures in the original 1937 edition are no longer recognized by contemporary readers. Certain examples and phrases seem as quaint and dated as those in a Victorian novel, weakening the overall impact of Carnegie's core message.

The Scope of the Revision
Our purpose in this revision is to clarify and strengthen the book for a modern reader without tampering with the fundamental content.

What Changed: Minor excisions were made, and a few contemporary examples were added.

What Stayed: The brash, breezy Carnegie style remains completely intact—even the 1930s slang is preserved.

Dale Carnegie wrote as he spoke: in an intensely exuberant, colloquial, and conversational manner. His voice still speaks as forcefully as ever, both in print and through Carnegie training courses worldwide.

To all readers, we offer this revision in the spirit of honing and polishing a finely made tool.

— Dorothy Carnegie (Mrs. Dale Carnegie)
